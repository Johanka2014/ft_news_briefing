UN Warns of 'Supersized' El Niño

"The science leaves no room for doubt: the planet is in uncharted waters, and those waters are heating up." - António Guterres

BBC News Report

The world has entered a "danger zone of extreme weather" as the impacts of El Niño start to be felt globally, UN head António Guterres has warned.

The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) has released the latest data on the natural weather phenomenon showing it could be the strongest for more than 70 years and last until at least February 2027.

El Niño is a naturally occurring phenomenon that happens over the Pacific Ocean roughly every two to seven years and can push up global temperatures. In normal conditions, trade winds take warm water from South America towards Asia. During an El Niño, these winds weaken or reverse, allowing warmer water to spread across the central and eastern Pacific.

This event changes weather patterns around the world. Hot, dry weather may hit parts of South America, South East Asia and Australia, increasing the risk of droughts and wildfires. Conversely, wetter-than-average weather is expected in parts of eastern Africa and the southern US, raising the chances of flooding.

The impacts are already being felt. The WMO has undertaken a "major mobilisation" to circulate forecasts to communities. In Panama, the number of vessels passing through the canal is being reduced due to lower rainfall. Indonesia is deploying cloud-seeding aircraft to extinguish widespread wildfires, and Australia has introduced early warning systems for marine crises to protect species from unprecedented ocean heat.

Experts note that this is happening on top of long-term human-caused warming. Miguel Aréstegui from Practical Action states that while the event is concerning, we must focus on preparedness. He stresses that El Niño "does not arrive in a vacuum," highlighting that structural issues like fragile sanitation systems and housing in exposed areas exacerbate these disasters.
